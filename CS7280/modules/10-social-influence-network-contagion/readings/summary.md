# Lesson 10 Recommended Reading — Topic Summary

> Source: CS-7280-O01, OAN (Summer 2026) — Lesson 10 supplementary readings

## Summary

The Lesson 10 readings supply the empirical evidence and the algorithmic theory behind the lecture's treatment of social influence and information cascades. Three of them are large-scale measurement studies of real online diffusion — two by Bakshy and collaborators on Facebook and Twitter, plus the Christakis–Fowler book that frames the whole "behaviors spread through social ties" thesis — and they ground the lesson's claims that adoption follows a diminishing-returns curve, that the overwhelming majority of cascades die at the source while a heavy tail grows enormous, and that abundant weak ties carry novel information. The remaining two readings supply the formal backbone: Kempe, Kleinberg and Tardos prove that the cascade-size objective is monotone and submodular and that the greedy seeding heuristic therefore achieves a $(1 - 1/e)$ approximation, while Ohtsuki et al. give the evolutionary-game-theory counterpart showing when cooperation survives on a network.

The unifying thread is that influence on a network is governed by structure, not just by individual persuasiveness. Whether a behavior, opinion, or piece of information takes off depends on the topology around the seed nodes, the thresholds of the adopters, the density of the communities the cascade must cross, and — in the cooperation setting — the benefit-to-cost ratio relative to node degree. The measurement papers and the theory papers meet at the same conclusion the lecture reaches analytically: dense clusters with density greater than $1-\theta$ block cascades, the seeding problem is NP-hard but well-approximable because of submodularity, and simple local rules (a degree-scaled $b/c$ threshold) decide global outcomes.

The key takeaway: the models introduced in the lesson — Linear Threshold, Independent Contagion, Deffuant, the coordination game, and the Asynchronous Linear Threshold (ALT) model applied to brain networks — are not just toy abstractions. Each reading shows one of them validated against data or sharpened into a provable guarantee, which is why the lesson can move from intuition to the submodularity result and the cluster-density theorem.

## Details

- **The role of social networks in information diffusion.** [[role-of-social-networks-in-information-diffusion.pdf]] (Bakshy, Rosenn, Marlow, Adamic, WWW 2012) reports a randomized experiment on 253 million Facebook users that turned feed exposure to friends' shares on and off. Exposed users were significantly more likely to share, and sooner; the probability of sharing rises **concavely** with the number of sharing friends, supporting the diminishing-returns model. Crucially, although strong ties are individually more influential, the far more numerous **weak ties** account for most of the spread of novel information — the source of the lesson's strong-vs-weak-tie discussion and the feed/no-feed figure.
- **Everyone's an influencer: quantifying influence on Twitter.** [[everyones-an-influencer-quantifying-influence-twitter.pdf]] (Bakshy, Hofman, Mason, Watts, WSDM 2011) tracks 74 million URL-mentioning tweet cascades from 1.6 million seed users. About **90%** of tweets trigger no cascade at all and another **9%** reach only one other user, yet a heavy tail produces cascades exceeding 1000 users and depth 8+. Cascade **size** is power-law distributed and cascade **depth** decays exponentially. The paper's attempt to predict influence shows that the largest cascades are individually unpredictable, so cost-effective seeding targets many ordinary "influencers" rather than a few celebrities — the empirical basis for the lesson's size/depth distributions.
- **Maximizing the spread of influence through a social network.** [[maximizing-spread-of-influence-social-network.pdf]] (Kempe, Kleinberg, Tardos, KDD 2003) formalizes **influence maximization**: choose $k$ seed nodes to maximize the expected cascade $f(S)$ under the Linear Threshold or Independent Cascade model. They prove $f$ is monotone and **submodular**, so the Nemhauser greedy algorithm gives a $(1 - 1/e) \approx 0.63$ approximation, while exact optimization is NP-hard. This is exactly the result the "Seeding", "Submodularity", and "Monotone and Submodular Function" sections develop, including the live-edge argument for submodularity of the Independent Cascade objective.
- **A simple rule for the evolution of cooperation on graphs and social networks.** [[simple-rule-evolution-cooperation-graphs.pdf]] (Ohtsuki, Hauert, Lieberman, Nowak, Nature 2006) studies the coordination game of the "Game Theoretic Diffusion Models" section on graphs. Their central result is the compact rule that cooperation is favored when the benefit-to-cost ratio exceeds the average degree, $b/c > k$ — a structural condition explaining why, contrary to the lesson's simplest single-defector argument, cooperation can persist on sparse networks.
- **Multisensory integration in the mouse cortical connectome using a network diffusion model.** [[network-diffusion-mouse-cortical-connectome.pdf]] (Shadi, Dyer, Dovrolis, Network Neuroscience 2020) applies the **Asynchronous Linear Threshold (ALT)** model — weighted by projection density, delayed by physical distance — to the mouse cortical connectome, reproducing observed multisensory integration patterns. It is the case study behind the "Network Diffusion in the Brain", "ALT Model", and "Experimental Validation of ALT Model" sections.
- **Connected (book review).** [[connected-christakis-fowler-book-review.pdf]] reviews Christakis and Fowler's *Connected: The Surprising Power of our Social Networks and How they Shape our Lives*, the popular-science synthesis cited on the "Not Just Viruses Spread On Networks" page for the claim that behaviors — obesity, smoking, happiness — spread to two and three degrees of separation through the social network.

## Examples

- **Facebook feed experiment** — sharing probability climbs concavely from ~0.002 to ~0.02 as the number of sharing friends grows from 1 to 6, and the "feed" condition roughly triples the "no feed" condition at every point, demonstrating both diminishing returns and the causal effect of exposure.
- **Twitter cascade tail** — 90% of tweets stop at the source and 9% reach one user, but rare events reach thousands of users at depth 8+, giving the power-law size and exponential depth distributions.
- **Greedy seeding guarantee** — because $f(S)$ is submodular, picking the marginally best seed each round yields a cascade no worse than $(1 - 1/e)$ of the optimal $k$-seed cascade, the practical justification for greedy influence maximization.
- **Cooperation on graphs** — the $b/c > k$ rule predicts cooperator clusters survive on low-degree lattices and graphs but are wiped out on dense, well-mixed networks.
